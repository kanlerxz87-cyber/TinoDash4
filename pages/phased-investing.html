Phased investing and reinvestment risk

Phasing parks the sale proceeds in short Treasury bills for 24 or 36 months, then moves them into equities over 12 months. Reinvestment risk is the chance that the rate earned on parked cash, or the price at which it is put back to work, is worse than expected. It is shown beside every phased result.

What the test shows

  • Phasing helps in a 1929-type crash: the lump sum falls to $4,626,110; the 36-month path holds $13,126,280.
  • Phasing costs in the median: the 24-month path gives $16,211,010 against $20,199,660 for the lump sum, 19.7% lower. Rule 5 allows 5%, so the phased paths fail.
  • Reinvestment risk is small for the parked cash: when bill yields fall to 1% from year 4, the 24-month path changes by 0.28%.

Investing policy for the sale proceeds (draft; for attorney and CPA review)

This follows section 5 of DECISION_RULES.md. It is a draft and not investment advice; no amounts are decided here.

  1. Purpose: hold the proceeds for the household's stated goals and protect against a forced sale in a downturn.
  2. Liquidity reserve: [placeholder: months of household spending, to be set by the household before funds arrive].
  3. Staging, if phasing is chosen: equal monthly tranches into diversified index exposure over the period the household chooses. The rules say 24 to 36 months; the model tests 12 (Flagged F-21).
  4. Rebalancing: once a year, to target weights set in advance.
  5. No forced sales: do not sell equities to fund an emergency while the reserve is intact.
  6. Review: annually, with the model re-run and the Flagged items checked.
  7. Tax coordination: the CPA confirms the holding structure and any trust or entity wrapper before funds are invested.
