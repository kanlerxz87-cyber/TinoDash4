Taxes, separated properly

Estate tax, income-tax basis, and property-tax assessment are three unrelated numbers. Keeping them apart is most of the work.

Three different taxes, three different bases, constantly confused

The single most useful thing this site can do is keep four quantities apart. They have different bases, different rates, different authorities and different dates. Conflating them is how households make expensive decisions.

Why this matters immediately

The household's biggest stated worry is the inheritance exceeding a tax-free threshold. For a California resident, that threshold is federal only, it is $15,000,000 per person in 2026, and it is now permanent with inflation indexing. Selling the Cupertino house does not change it by one dollar — the house is inside the estate whether it is a house or a brokerage account. Meanwhile the tax that does change with the decision is the income tax on the sale, and it is smaller if you sell promptly and larger if you wait.

If the estate might exceed the exclusion, the fixes are before the death

Nothing in the sell-versus-hold decision reduces estate tax. The available tools are lifetime gifting against the annual exclusion ($19,000 per recipient in 2026), the marital deduction, charitable planning, and — where a closely held business or farm is involved — the deferred-payment election under § 6166, whose 2026 "2-percent portion" is $1,940,000. All of these require the decedent's cooperation while alive. This is why the question “ask now about portability and prior taxable gifts” is urgent and not a formality.

Portability, and the deadline that is easy to miss

A surviving spouse can inherit a deceased spouse's unused exclusion, but only if a Form 706 is filed. For estates that are not otherwise required to file, the IRS allows a simplified late election up to the fifth anniversary of the date of death (Rev. Proc. 2022-32). For an estate that is required to file, the deadline is 9 months after date of death (extendable) and the simplified extension is unavailable.

The practical point: an estate that owes no tax still has to file to preserve portability, and the cost of filing is small compared with the value of the exclusion. Decide this within nine months of the death, not five years later.

The income tax: where California's structure actually hurts

Federal long-term capital gains get preferential rates. California's do not. This asymmetry is the single largest tax fact in this file.

Federal

  • Long-term gains taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on taxable income (IRS Topic 409).
  • Plus a 3.8% net investment income tax above $250,000 MAGI for joint filers (IRS NIIT).
  • Gain on a principal residence excluded up to $250,000 single / $500,000 joint (IRS Topic 701), and the excluded portion is not subject to NIIT.
  • Unrecaptured § 1250 gain (depreciation on rental property) capped at 25%.

California

  • Capital gains taxed as ordinary income. No preference, no 0/15/20 split.
  • Top marginal rate 12.3%.
  • Plus 1% Behavioral Health Services Tax on taxable income above $1,000,000 (" Form 540 line 62 ""), giving a 13.3% top marginal rate.
  • The $1,000,000 threshold is not indexed and is the same for married couples filing jointly. A single large gain can cross it on its own.

The 1% tax the household asked me to read the actual instructions for

Confirmed directly from the California Franchise Tax Board's 2025 Form 540 booklet, line 62: “If your taxable income is more than $1,000,000, compute the Behavioral Health Services Tax using whole dollars only: taxable income from Form 540 line 19, less $(1,000,000), times 1%.” It was formerly called the Mental Health Services Tax; the mechanics are unchanged. It applies to the excess only, it is not subject to reduction by credits, and it is subject to the estimated-tax payment requirement.

Practical consequence for this household: if the sale of the Cupertino house pushes California taxable income over $1,000,000 in the sale year, the entire gain is also taxed at the 12.3% top bracket, and the 1% surtax applies on top. Splitting the sale across two tax years, or timing it in a lower-income year, is worth more than almost any other lever in this file.

What Proposition 19 is actually worth here — and the counter-intuitive part

Conventional wisdom says an inherited California tax base is worth a fortune. The model says something more interesting: for a transfer around 2045 it is worth much less than people assume, because by then the house has grown so far above its base year value that the capped exclusion protects only a sliver of it.

Proposition 19 does not let the heir keep the parent's base year value. It lets them keep it plus the inflation-adjusted allowance — $1,044,590 for transfers through February 2027. Everything above that is added back to the assessment. By 2045 the house is worth far more than base plus allowance, so most of the value is reassessed either way. The protection is a cap on reassessment, not an exemption from it.

Why the folklore is out of date

Before February 2021, Proposition 58 let a child inherit a parent's base year value with no value limit at all. That is where the “never sell, the tax base is life-changing” stories come from, and they were true. Proposition 19 replaced it with a capped version, and for a house in this value range the cap binds hard. The stories did not stop when the law changed.

Separately: the structure the folklore describes does still apply to Property B, whose assessed value is roughly one-fifteenth of its market value after decades of Proposition 13. That is real. It simply belongs to the building already owned, not to the one being inherited.

The trust question, answered

The household asked whether inheriting through a trust changes the analysis. On the property-tax side, no — RTC § 63.2(e)(9) expressly includes transfers of present beneficial ownership through an inter vivos or testamentary trust. On the income-tax side, also generally no: the § 1014 basis step-up applies to property acquired from a decedent, and property distributed from a trust that is included in the decedent's gross estate gets the same treatment. What a trust does change is control, timing of distribution, and whether the one-year occupancy clock starts on the date of death or the date of distribution — which is a question for the attorney, and is on the open questions list.
