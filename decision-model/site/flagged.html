Nothing on this page blocks the analysis, and none of it is hidden. It is the honest inventory of what this model does not know, what it guessed, and what it found that looked wrong. Review it before relying on any conclusion.

The three items that matter most

  1. The $3,100,000 valuation is unverified. It came from the household, not an appraisal, and every path in the model is denominated in it.
  2. Property B's effective tax rate looks wrong. About 2.1% of assessed value against a 1% constitutional base, with a tax bill that fell 5.9% in 2021 while the assessment rose 1%. The household's series may compare two different parcels or two different years.
  3. The response to the one-year occupancy deadline is unknown. The entire Prop 19 advantage depends on it, and it is a legal test rather than a forecast.

Irregularities found while verifying

These are things that did not reconcile. They are recorded rather than smoothed over, because a model that quietly resolves an inconsistency is worse than one that reports it.

1. Property B: a 2.1% effective tax rate against a 1% constitutional base

Roughly $2,400 of annual tax on roughly $114,000 of assessed value implies an effective rate near 2.1%. Property A's own series implies 1.2%–1.4%. The Proposition 13 base rate is 1%, plus voter-approved debt and fixed charges, which in practice runs to about 1.2%–1.4% in most Santa Clara County tax rate areas. A figure of 2.1% is roughly double.

Additional inconsistency: the household's San Francisco series shows the tax bill falling 5.9% in 2021 while the assessed value rose 1%. Under a 1%-plus-debt formula that cannot happen unless the debt component changed, which would be unusual.

Possible explanations: (a) the tax and assessment columns describe different parcels or different fiscal years; (b) there are large special assessments or a Mello-Roos district on the parcel; (c) the property is in a redevelopment area with a substantial debt service component. These have different consequences — (b) and (c) are real recurring costs missing from the Property B model.

Resolution: the two most recent secured property tax bills and the assessor's parcel page.

2. The assessed-value and tax columns do not quite reconcile in either property

In both series, multiplying the stated assessed value by the implied rate does not reproduce the stated tax to the dollar in every year. That is expected — the effective rate varies with the debt levy — but the size of the variation in the San Francisco series is not consistent with a stable parcel. Both series should be read off the actual bills rather than inferred.

3. California's absence of an estate tax could not be confirmed from a single official page

The conclusion is not in doubt in practice — California repealed its inheritance and gift tax in 1982, and the FTB's own Form 540 has no estate-tax line. But I could not fetch an FTB page that states it in those words, and the instruction for this project was to mark anything not confirmed as unverified rather than infer it. It is therefore published as secondary with the flag recorded, and flagged for the attorney to confirm in one sentence.

4. San Francisco Proposition H's passage is confirmed by reporters, not by the canvass

Four independent news organisations reported the measure passing with about 72% of the vote on 3 November 2026. I did not fetch the Department of Elections' official results page. The dollar consequence for a three-unit building is roughly $249 a year, so the materiality is low — but the passage is recorded as secondary rather than verified, and the official canvass should be checked.

5. The Santa Clara County transfer tax rate is from secondary compilations

$1.10 per $1,000 of consideration is consistent across a 2019 California Lawyers Association compilation, several secondary sources, and Revenue & Taxation Code § 11911's base rate. I could not fetch an official Santa Clara County fee schedule in this session. On a $3,100,000 sale the tax is about $3,410 — immaterial to the decision, which is why it was not pursued further, but it remains unverified.

6. Property A city conveyance tax: possibly none, unverified

San Jose, Palo Alto and Mountain View impose an additional city conveyance tax of $1.65 per $500. I found no evidence of an equivalent Property A tax. Absence of evidence is not verification; confirm with the Property A's city finance department.

7. The § 121 exclusion assumes a joint return and met tests

The model applies the $500,000 exclusion. If the heir files singly, it is $250,000. If the two-of-five ownership and use tests are not met — which is exactly what happens if the household moves out — the exclusion is zero, not reduced. The model's live-then-rent path shows this consequence.

8. Maintenance modelling hides lumpy capital events

At 2% of building value, the model produces a smooth annual number. Real houses do not work that way: a roof is $40,000 once, not $4,000 ten times. The smooth average is reasonable over 30 years and wrong in any single year, which matters if the household's cash flow is tight in a particular year.

9. The crash magnitudes are standard historical figures, not individually re-verified

The NBER contraction dates are verified from NBER. The drawdown magnitudes (89% for 1929, 57% for 2008, and so on) are the widely reported figures and are used as modelling inputs. They were not each re-derived from a primary statistical release in this session. They are labelled as modelling inputs on the assumptions page.

10. No legislation tracking was performed

The household asked that proposed legislation be tracked and that enacted changes be distinguished from proposals. This site does neither — it records only enacted law as of 2026-11-25. Nothing is presented as a proposal, and nothing is presented as likely. The authorities page says so explicitly. Setting up a quarterly check is on the open questions list.

How to request a change

Every flagged item has a file behind it. Corrections are one pull request each: edit data/facts.json, rebuild, and the site regenerates. The suggestions page describes the workflow.

This is a decision-support model, not legal, tax or investment advice. Every figure traces to a verified authority or an explicit assumption. Unverified items are on Flagged for review. Confirm everything with a California estate attorney, a CPA, and a fee-only fiduciary adviser before acting.
