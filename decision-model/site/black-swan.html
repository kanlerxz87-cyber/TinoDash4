Stress tests & black swans

Five macro regimes, six crash overlays, and a direct test of the proposed 24–36 month Treasury parking strategy.

Why this page exists

A single smooth growth rate over fifty years is not a projection, it is a straight line with a story attached. Growth and inflation are never consistent over 50 years. The NBER's own record of U.S. business cycles shows 34 contractions since 1854, lasting from 6 to 65 months. The household's instinct — that a 50-year plan must survive 1929 and 2008 — is correct, and this page tests it.

The macro regimes

Five named regimes, not one. Each is internally consistent: the stagflation case has high inflation and low real equity returns, because that is what the 1970s were. A base case with 7% equity and 2.5% inflation is deliberately set below the post-1980 U.S. average, because the household asked for realism rather than optimism.

What the grid shows

  • Every path loses in 1929. The differences between paths shrink in relative terms but the absolute ordering is stable. Selling still wins.
  • Holding is comparatively better in a crash, not worse, because Proposition 8 lets the assessment fall with the market, cutting property tax, while a portfolio has no such relief. The house is a partially indexed asset.
  • The 1973 case is the interesting one. Stocks fall and inflation accelerates. The house, being a real asset financed with a nominal tax, does relatively well. This is the scenario in which holding is closest to competitive.
  • The 2022 case breaks the phased-investing story, and is the reason the next section exists.

Phased investing: the household's specific question

The proposal was to park the sale proceeds in 3-month to 2-year Treasuries for 24–36 months after the inheritance, then phase into stocks, to soften crash-entry risk. This section tests whether that works. It is run on the sell path, because that is the path that actually faces a single-day entry into the market.

The break-even is the key number. Parking the proceeds for 2 years costs about 4.2% of terminal value. To be worth it, the crash avoided must be larger than about 3.9% — which 1929, 2008 and 2000 all were, and a garden-variety 10% correction is not.

Now the honest version: the crash does not have to arrive on schedule

If every crash is assumed to arrive one year after the transfer, then any parking window longer than the crash duration “works” by construction. That is a tautology. The finding, in one paragraph

Phasing is cheap insurance against a crash in the first three years, and a pure cost against everything else. If a 1929 or 2008 arrives in the first three years after the inheritance, parking the proceeds in Treasuries is worth $14.98M to $13.39M of terminal net worth. If no crash arrives — the overwhelmingly likely case in any given three-year window — it costs $2.80M, which is the equity risk premium forgone. Over a 30-year horizon the expected value of phasing is negative, and the household should buy it for the sleep, not for the return.

What the household should actually do instead

If the goal is to reduce entry risk, phasing over 36 months is a weaker tool than simply not selling all at once. Three better instruments exist, and they are compared on the reinvestment risk page:

  1. A written investment policy with a rebalancing band. This is the real protection against behavioural failure, and it costs nothing.
  2. Holding 2 years of spending in cash or T-bills so that a crash never forces a sale. This eliminates the actual ruin scenario, which is not a drawdown but a drawdown plus a forced liquidation.
  3. Not selling the house in one tax year, if the gain is large — an installment sale or a sale spread across tax years manages the California tax, which is a bigger number than the entry-timing risk.

Beyond the crash: the risks a scenario grid cannot show

Single-industry concentration

Property A's property values are tied to one industry's willingness to employ people at high salaries within commuting distance. A durable shift to distributed work or a sector contraction hits this asset directly and hits a diversified portfolio only partly. The tech-bust regime in the table models this.

Insurance withdrawal

California's admitted market has contracted sharply in wildfire-exposed areas. A $3.1M house that cannot be insured at any reasonable price is a different asset. The model carries a $1,500–$4,500 range; the true downside is not a price, it is uninsurability.

Legislative change

Proposition 19 itself was a 2020 ballot measure that removed a benefit families had relied on for 35 years. A future ballot measure could restore it, narrow it further, or create a new one. Nothing in this model is safe from that, and the authorities page tracks what is enacted versus merely proposed.
