Property B — the existing three-unit building

The building the household already owns. It changes the risk of the Property A decision more than any single assumption in the model.

Why the San Francisco building is not a side note

The household was clear that the Property A house is being inherited while the San Francisco building is already owned. That distinction is load-bearing, and it cuts in two directions at once: Property B makes the household more able to hold Property A, and it makes holding it more dangerous.

Why it makes holding Property A easier

  • A $7,150/month rent roll on a building that is nearly paid off means the household does not need Property A to generate cash. It can be held purely as a store of value.
  • The household can live in one of the three units, which means it already has a residence in San Francisco — so the choice about Property A is a genuine choice about where to live, not a forced one.
  • Property B's low assessed value is a demonstration of what the Prop 13 basis is worth over decades, which is a real argument for trying to preserve Property A's basis too.

Why it makes holding Property A more dangerous

  • Concentration. Two Bay Area residential properties plus a large cash inheritance is not a diversified balance sheet. It is a leveraged bet on one region and one industry. Adding Property A doubles down on the bet that already exists.
  • Prop 19 is gone for Property B. If Property B was inherited or transferred after 16 February 2021 without the heir occupying it, it was reassessed to market. Its very low assessed value implies it was acquired or transferred long ago — worth confirming, because if it was transferred recently and should have been reassessed, there may be a supplemental assessment waiting.
  • Three units, two tenancies, one vacancy. That is a management obligation, not a passive asset. Adding a second rental property to a household that is already a landlord compounds operational risk.
  • Liquidity. Neither property can be sold quickly. If the household needs cash, the only liquid asset is the inheritance itself.

The one question about Property B that most changes the Property A decision

Is the third unit legal and habitable? If it is, Property B has unrealised income, the household has a genuine choice of where to live, and the Property A hold path becomes a lifestyle preference rather than a financial necessity. If it is not a legal unit, the building is a two-unit for planning and rent-control purposes, the value estimate is too high, and the household has fewer options than it thinks. One trip to the Planning Department settles it.
