Reinvestment risk & the investing policy

The sell recommendation is only as good as the reinvestment that follows it. The arithmetic of the parking window, and a policy that has to be written before the money arrives.

Reinvestment risk, stated next to the headline

Every projection on this site that ends with a large number assumes the money gets reinvested. That assumption is doing enormous work and it deserves its own page, because it is the one assumption the household controls completely and the one most likely to fail.

The number behind the sell recommendation

The sell path's advantage over live-and-hold is roughly $23.18M over 30 years. All of it depends on the after-tax proceeds being invested at the modelled equity return for three decades. If the proceeds sit in a savings account, or are spent, or are invested and then sold in a panic in year 12, the advantage disappears and the recommendation was wrong. This is a modelling result, not a prediction.

Why the break-even is lower than intuition suggests

A naive calculation says “parking costs 2% a year, so I need a 20% crash to break even.” That is wrong, because the crashed portfolio then recovers from a lower base and the un-crashed one does not. The correct comparison compounds both forward to the horizon. Doing it properly puts the break-even near 3.9% — much lower than intuition, which is genuinely an argument for some parking. But read it together with the timing table on Black swans: the break-even only matters if the crash lands inside the window.

The investment policy statement

The household asked for a documented investing policy. This is a template to be completed before the money arrives, because a policy written after a 40% drawdown is not a policy, it is a rationalisation. It is deliberately short enough that it will actually be re-read.

Investment Policy Statement — Property A proceeds

Purpose of the moneyTo replace the income and shelter the inherited house would have provided, for a horizon of at least 30 years, without requiring a sale during a drawdown.
Return objectiveLong-run nominal return of inflation + 3.5%, net of fees, measured over rolling 15-year periods. Not measured annually.
Risk objectiveAccept a peak-to-trough decline of up to 50% without changing the policy. Write down now what you will do at −20%, −35% and −50%. The answer must be “nothing” for each.
Liquidity reserveHold 2 years of household spending in cash or T-bills, outside the invested portfolio. This is the single most important line in this document, because it removes the only scenario in which a drawdown becomes permanent.
Asset allocationGlobal equity 60–80%, investment-grade bonds 15–35%, cash 2 years of spending. Rebalance when any sleeve drifts more than 5 percentage points from target. Rebalancing is mechanical; it is not a market call.
What is forbiddenIndividual stocks (the household already holds enormous single-name exposure through Property A and through the San Francisco building). Leverage. Anything with a lock-up longer than the liquidity reserve. Concentrated positions of any kind.
Phasing ruleIf the proceeds arrive as a lump sum, invest over 12 months by default, not 36. Phasing beyond a year is a cost, not a protection — see the timing table. Deviate only if a written note explains why, dated before the deviation.
Review cadenceAnnually, on a fixed date. Review the policy, not the performance. Performance is reviewed only over rolling 15-year windows.
Who decidesNamed in advance. Successor named in advance. No changes without a written, dated amendment.

Template only. Not investment advice. Complete it with a fee-only fiduciary.

What reinvestment risk looks like when it goes wrong

Three failure modes, each of which turns the sell recommendation into a loss. The model cannot prevent any of them.

Cash drag

The proceeds sit in a money-market fund “waiting for a good entry point.” The model can price that: running the sell path with a 5-percentage-point shortfall in the equity return turns $38.35M into roughly $9.06M. This is the most common failure and it does not feel like a decision at the time.

Panic sale

A 50% drawdown in year 8 of a 30-year plan, followed by a sale to “stop the bleeding,” locks in the loss and removes the recovery. The 1929 scenario shows the geometric cost. The liquidity reserve exists to make this unnecessary.

Lifestyle absorption

The money is reinvested but the spending rises to meet it. A $2.5M portfolio supports roughly $100,000/yr at a 4% rate; a household that spends $150,000/yr is consuming principal and the projection was fiction from year one.
