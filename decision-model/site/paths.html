Why sell scores highest, stated plainly

Not because the model dislikes houses. Because of three arithmetic facts.

1. Tax in California has no long-term rate

California taxes capital gains as ordinary income, at up to 12.3%, plus the 1% Behavioral Health Services Tax above $1,000,000 of taxable income. There is no state equivalent of the federal 0/15/20 brackets. A large gain is therefore taxed at roughly the same rate as salary.

2. The step-up is worth more than the exclusion

The § 1014 basis reset at death means the gain realized in the sale year is close to zero. The tax cost of selling promptly is mostly the ~10% transaction cost, not capital-gains tax. Delay the sale and you manufacture a taxable gain that did not have to exist.

3. A house costs money every year; a portfolio costs almost nothing

Property tax at roughly 1.3% of assessed value, insurance, maintenance at 2% of building value, water: together a real, recurring, non-diversified call on cash flow. A portfolio has an expense ratio.

Sell at transfer, invest the net proceeds

Base case$38,351,710
Range$38.07M – $38.64M
Cash generated$0
Tax on final sale$0

Accept the step-up in basis and sell in the year of death, then invest the after-tax proceeds in a diversified portfolio. Because the § 1014 basis IS the date-of-death value, the gain in that same year is normally close to zero — the real cost is the 10%-plus transaction cost, not capital-gains tax.

Legal condition. Selling later, after appreciation, creates real gain taxed at California ordinary rates with no state preference at all. The timing of the sale, not the sale itself, is the tax decision.

Risk the number does not capture. Concentrates the entire entry into the market on a single day. This is the path most exposed to a bad entry date, and it is the reason the phased-investing option exists.

Live in it 6 years, then sell using the § 121 exclusion

Base case$31,226,550
Range$30.58M – $31.90M
Cash generated$338,440
Tax on final sale$313,550

Occupy and claim Prop 19, satisfy the § 121 ownership-and-use tests, then sell with up to $500,000 of gain excluded from federal income. Because the basis was reset at death and the house then grew, this is where the § 121 exclusion earns its keep.

Legal condition. § 121 requires ownership for 2 of the last 5 years AND use as the main home for 2 of the last 5 (IRS Topic 701). Gain above the exclusion is taxed at federal 0/15/20 plus 3.8% NIIT, and at California ordinary rates.

Risk the number does not capture. Market timing on the exit date; six years is a long time to be wrong about a neighbourhood.

Live in it as a principal residence, hold to horizon

Base case$15,418,500
Range$12.50M – $18.52M
Cash generated$2.80M
Tax on final sale$3,651,210

Move in within one year, file the homeowners' exemption within one year, claim the Prop 19 exclusion, and stay. Lowest property tax of any holding path. Credited with imputed rent equal to the market rent the household no longer has to pay.

Legal condition. Requires actual, continuing occupancy. The exclusion is removed when the heir stops being eligible for the homeowners' exemption. Living there is not a lifestyle preference in this model — it is the legal condition of the tax benefit.

Risk the number does not capture. Locks roughly $3.1M of today's capital into one undiversified asset in a single-industry town, on top of an already-concentrated San Francisco rental. The concentration, not the return, is the risk.

Rent it for one year, then move in and claim Prop 19

Base case$15,389,610
Range$12.47M – $18.47M
Cash generated$2.79M
Tax on final sale$3,672,130

Use the house as a rental during the transition year, then occupy within the one-year window that § 63.2(a)(1)(A) allows, preserving the Prop 19 exclusion.

Legal condition. Legal but narrow. The occupancy deadline runs one year from the TRANSFER, not one year from the end of a lease. A twelve-month lease that overruns the window destroys the exclusion and reassesses the property to full market value.

Risk the number does not capture. A twelve-month tenancy creates a relationship you may have to unwind, and it changes the character of the residence timeline.

Live in it, then rent it out later

Base case$13,657,580
Range$10.79M – $15.99M
Cash generated$1.67M
Tax on final sale$4,282,870

Occupy long enough to establish the residence and claim the exclusion, then convert to rental when the household moves. The model ENFORCES the loss of the exclusion at conversion.

Legal condition. THE TRAP. RTC § 63.2(a)(1)(B)-(C): the exclusion is removed on the date the transferee is no longer eligible for the homeowners' exemption, and a new base year value is enrolled as of the following lien date. Moving out and renting does not 'keep' a Prop 13 basis — it forfeits it, converting a low-tax house into a full-market-value one, retroactively. This path is modelled specifically to put that number in front of the household before they rely on it.

Risk the number does not capture. The Prop 19 benefit is destroyed at the moment of conversion, which is usually the exact moment the plan was counting on the low tax base to make renting profitable.

Range$10.79M – $15.69M
Cash generated$1.73M

Treat the house purely as an investment property. Under RTC § 63.2 the Prop 19 exclusion is unavailable because the heir does not occupy within one year, so the property is reassessed to full market value at transfer — which on a $3.1M house in 2045 is a six-figure annual tax bill against a five-figure rent roll.

Legal condition. This is the path the household's instinct most wants to exist — 'rent it out and keep the low Prop 13 base'. It does not. A low Prop 13 base and rental income are mutually exclusive under current California law, and the model charges the full cost of that.

Risk the number does not capture. Highest property tax of any holding path, income taxed as ordinary income, depreciation recapture on sale, and landlord obligations in a county with strong tenant protections.

The paths that do not exist, and why it matters that people think they do

“Rent it out and keep the Prop 13 base”

This is the most common belief about inherited California homes and it is wrong for transfers on or after 16 February 2021. Under RTC § 63.2 the parent-child exclusion requires the heir to occupy the home as a principal residence within one year, file the homeowners' exemption within one year, and continue to be eligible for that exemption. The exclusion is removed when eligibility ends, and a new base year value is enrolled as of the following lien date.

The model charges this explicitly: live-then-rent reassesses the property to market value at the moment of conversion. Compare it with live — the only difference between the two paths is that one move, and it costs $1,760,920 of terminal net worth.

But be careful about how much that sounds like. That figure is small precisely because this inheritance is projected for 2045, by which time the house has grown far above its base year value and Proposition 19's capped protection is protecting very little. The model computes the exclusion's whole value at $561,920 across the 30-year horizon — real money, and worth having, but only about 2% of the gap to selling. See what Prop 19 is really worth.

RTC § 63.2(a)(1)(B)–(C), official text

“Do a 1031 exchange into something else”

A like-kind exchange defers gain. But after a death there is no gain to defer — the basis was just stepped up to market. A § 1031 exchange in this situation defers nothing, carries the old low basis forward instead of getting a fresh one, and adds a 45/180-day deadline and a qualified intermediary. It is a solution to a problem the household does not have. It would only become relevant if the household held the house for years, rented it, and then wanted to exchange — by which point the Prop 19 reassessment has already happened.
