Inheritance, step by step

Twenty-one steps, in the order they must happen, with the three order-dependent traps called out.

The process, in the order it actually has to happen

The household asked for this step by step, and the ordering matters more than any individual step. Several of these are one-way doors, and at least two of them are routinely walked through in the wrong order by families who are trying to be efficient.

Before the death — while the trust can still be changed

  1. 1

    Confirm the trust actually holds the house

    A house titled in the parents' names personally, or in a joint tenancy, does not pass under the trust. If it is not in the trust, the analysis changes from a trust distribution to a probate or a joint-tenancy transfer, and the timelines in this section do not apply.

    Who: Attorney; the deed and the trust schedule of assets

  2. 2

    Confirm the trust is the one that will be used

    An older trust that names a different successor trustee, or that was superseded, is a common failure. Ask for the currently operative document and the most recent amendment.

    Who: Attorney; original trust and any amendments

  3. 3

    Ask about portability and prior taxable gifts

    This is the single most time-critical pre-death question. If the first spouse has already died and no Form 706 was filed, the portability election may still be available under Rev. Proc. 2022-32 up to five years after that death — but not after. Prior taxable gifts reduce the exclusion and must be known.

    Who: CPA; Forms 709 and 706, if any

  4. 4

    Get the basis record and the capital-improvement record

    The § 1014 basis will be the value at death, so the historical cost basis matters less than it seems — but capital improvements after the death DO add to basis, and the trust's records are far easier to obtain now than after the trustee changes.

    Who: Trustee; receipts, permits, contractor invoices

  5. 5

    Decide whether the house stays in trust or is distributed outright

    This is the fork in the road. A continuing trust can hold the house, defer decisions, and protect against a beneficiary's creditors or divorce. An outright distribution gives the heir control and starts the Prop 19 clocks. The choice is the settlor's to make, and it is much cheaper to make it now.

    Who: Attorney

  6. 6

    Check the estate against the federal exclusion

    The federal exclusion is $15,000,000 per person in 2026. California has no estate tax. If the projected estate is anywhere near the exclusion, planning now is worth far more than any decision made after the death.

    Who: CPA; a projected estate balance sheet

At the death — the first 30 days

  1. 7

    Obtain the death certificate

    Everything downstream needs it. Order at least a dozen certified copies.

    Who: County clerk-recorder

  2. 8

    Establish the date-of-death fair market value

    This single number is simultaneously (a) the § 1014 income-tax basis, (b) the Prop 19 value test, and (c) the estate-tax valuation for Form 706. Get it in writing, from a licensed appraiser, dated to the date of death. A retrospective appraisal is legitimate and normal; a broker's opinion from six months later is not the same thing.

    Who: Licensed appraiser (see questions.html#q-value)

  3. 9

    Secure the property and notify the insurer

    An unoccupied house in a high-value area needs a vacancy endorsement or the policy may not respond. This is a real, immediate, cheap step that people skip.

    Who: Insurance agent

  4. 10

    Notify the trustee and confirm authority

    The successor trustee needs a certified copy of the trust and often a certificate of trust to act with banks, insurers and the assessor.

    Who: Attorney

  5. 11

    Locate the deed, the title report and the property-tax bills

    The assessor's records and the prior owner's homeowners' exemption are what make the Prop 19 claim possible.

    Who: Title company; county assessor

  6. 12

    Do not change the deed yet

    Recording a deed before the tax and Prop 19 analysis is done can create a transfer-tax event, start clocks, and occasionally destroy the very exclusion the household wants. Sequence matters and this is the step where it is most often lost.

    Who: Attorney

Within the first year — the clocks that actually expire

  1. 13

    Occupy the home within one year of the transfer

    RTC § 63.2(a)(1)(A). This is a hard deadline and it is the one that decides the entire analysis. If the plan involves renting the house out for more than a year, the Prop 19 exclusion is gone.

    Who: Self; but confirm the exact start date (death vs distribution) with the attorney

  2. 14

    File the homeowners' property tax exemption within one year

    RTC § 63.2(a)(1)(B) and BOE-266. File by February 15 for the full year's benefit. This is $7,000 of assessed value — trivial money — but it is the legal trigger for Prop 19, so it is not optional.

    Who: County assessor, form BOE-266

  3. 15

    File the Prop 19 exclusion claim, form BOE-19-P

    The claim itself has a three-year window, but it cannot be filed until the occupancy and exemption conditions are met. File it early. Late filing can attract a processing fee of up to $175.

    Who: County assessor, form BOE-19-P

  4. 16

    Decide on Form 706 and portability

    Due nine months after death, extendable to fifteen. If the estate is below the exclusion there is no tax, but a filing may still be needed to elect portability. Rev. Proc. 2022-32 gives a simplified late election up to five years, but only for estates not otherwise required to file.

    Who: CPA; Form 706

  5. 17

    Get the property-tax bill and verify the assessment

    Do not assume the assessor got Prop 19 right. Assessors process these by hand and get them wrong in both directions. Reconcile the bill against the arithmetic: prior taxable value + max(0, FMV − (prior + allowance)).

    Who: County assessor

  6. 18

    Decide on insurance, in writing

    Whether the property is owner-occupied or a rental changes the policy, the premium and the coverage. Insurers withdraw from California markets without notice; start early.

    Who: Insurance broker

The year of the decision

  1. 19

    Decide occupancy before deciding anything else

    Not “probably”. This determines whether the property tax is roughly 1.3% of a half-million-dollar base or 1.3% of three million, which is a swing of tens of thousands of dollars a year.

    Who: Household; recorded in decision.html

  2. 20

    If selling: decide the tax year

    Selling in the year of death means the gain over the stepped-up basis is close to zero — the cost is the transaction cost. Selling later means appreciating into a taxable gain at California ordinary rates plus the 1% surtax above $1M. This is the highest-value timing decision in the file.

    Who: CPA

  3. 21

    If holding: get real operating numbers

    Three insurance quotes, a maintenance plan, a rent survey, and the actual water and electricity bills. The household's current estimates are placeholders.

    Who: Household; see questions.html

  4. 22

    If renting: model the recapture honestly

    Depreciation taken during the rental period is recaptured at up to 25% federal plus California ordinary rates on sale. The rent path's tax benefit is largely a deferral, not a saving.

    Who: CPA

  5. 23

    Write the decision down

    Which path, on what date, on what criteria. The decision-log template exists so that a future reader knows what was chosen and why.

    Who: Household; decision-log.html

Ongoing — the annual review

  1. 24

    Re-run the model every year with updated values

    The model is a script; it takes a minute. Values move, law moves, and a decision that was right in 2045 may not be right in 2055.

    Who: Household or adviser

  2. 25

    Recheck the official authorities

    The authorities page lists every source. Twenty minutes a year: the IRS exclusion, the BOE allowance, the SF Rent Board, the assessor's assessment.

    Who: Household

  3. 26

    Review the extreme scenarios annually

    Not to change the plan, but to confirm the plan still survives them. The 1929 and 2008 columns are the ones to look at.

    Who: Household

  4. 27

    Track proposed legislation and ballot measures

    Prop 19 removed a benefit families had relied on since 1986. Prop H added a parcel tax in 2026. Both were voted on, not decided by experts.

    Who: Household; authorities.html

The three order-dependent traps

1. Recording a deed before the analysis

A deed is a legal act and, depending on the consideration recited, can create a transfer-tax event and fix the date from which the Prop 19 clocks run. Do the analysis first; record once.

2. Renting the house “temporarily” while deciding

This is the most natural thing in the world and it is the single most expensive mistake available. A twelve-month lease takes the household past the one-year occupancy deadline. There is no grace period and no substantial-compliance doctrine in RTC § 63.2. The result is a reassessment to full market value that persists for as long as the property is owned.

3. Treating the trust as the owner instead of the heir

If the trust keeps the house and the heir lives in it, the occupancy and exemption tests have to be satisfied by whoever is the transferee. The distinction between “the trust distributes the house to the heir” and “the trust keeps the house and lets the heir live there” is decisive, and it is not obvious. This is the first question for the attorney.
